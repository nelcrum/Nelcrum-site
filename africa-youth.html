Africa's Youth Unemployment

A companion to The Wall Street Journal's charts on why young people are fed up with the economy, extending the same World Bank comparison to Africa.

By Dontá Council, PhDNovember 2026Data refreshed monthly, last on November 19, 2026

Key findings

  1. 16 of 51 African countries with estimates have higher youth unemployment than France (18.9%), the highest of the five countries in the Journal's charts.
  2. Low unemployment rates can mask precarious work. In Madagascar, youth unemployment is 5.2%, but 84.4% of workers are in vulnerable employment: self-employed without employees, or working unpaid in a family business.
  3. 40.4% of Sub-Saharan Africa's population is under 15, against 14.2% in the European Union.
About this analysis

On November 18, 2026, The Wall Street Journal published a set of charts on why young people around the world are frustrated with the economy, describing a generation that is fed up “in nearly every corner of the world.” The charts compared youth unemployment in France, China, the U.K., the U.S. and Germany, along with the share of young Europeans living with their parents.

Africa was not among the places charted, a gap readers noted as the graphics circulated on social media. Sub-Saharan Africa is the world's youngest region, with 40.4% of its population under 15, and the continent has seen several of the Gen Z protests, beginning with Kenya's in June 2024 and followed by Nigeria, Togo, Morocco, Madagascar, Cameroon and Tanzania.

This analysis keeps the Journal's framing and its data source, the World Bank's youth unemployment estimates, and adds the 51 African countries with estimates so the figures can be read side by side.

Of the five economies in the chart, France had the highest youth unemployment rate, at 18.9%. Among the 51 African countries with an estimate, 16 have a higher rate than France. South Africa's is 59.9%.

The whole continent

Ranking every African country with an estimate against the five in the original chart shows that 16 exceed the highest rate the Journal charted. Djibouti tops the list at 76.8%. The highest rates are concentrated in Southern Africa and North Africa, with the Republic of Congo, Gabon and Somalia also near the top. Many of the lowest are in West Africa, for reasons the next chart explains.

Low unemployment, high precarity

Five of the seven protest countries report youth unemployment below Germany's 6.9%: Tanzania (2.4%), Togo (3.4%), Madagascar (5.2%), Nigeria (5.3%) and Cameroon (6.5%). That does not necessarily signal a healthy labor market. The unemployment rate counts only people with no work at all who are looking for it. One likely reason is that where unemployment benefits are limited, few young people can afford to stay out of work while they search, so many take whatever informal work exists.

In Madagascar, youth-led protests ended Andry Rajoelina's presidency in October 2025, yet youth unemployment is 5.2% while 84.4% of all workers are in vulnerable employment: working for themselves or unpaid in a family business, usually without a contract or social protection.

What is vulnerable employment?

Vulnerable employment is the share of all workers who are either own-account workers, self-employed with no employees (a street vendor, a smallholder farmer, a motorbike-taxi driver), or contributing family workers, who work unpaid in a business or farm run by a relative.

The International Labour Organization treats it as a gauge of precarious work. The World Bank's notes on the indicator describe these workers as the least likely to have formal work arrangements or social protection, and the least protected against economic shocks (World Bank definition).

It measures how people work, not how much they earn, and it covers workers of all ages, not only the young. A self-employed professional counts too, so treat it as an indicator of precarious work.

The youngest region on earth

In Sub-Saharan Africa, 40.4% of the population is under 15. In the European Union the figure is 14.2%. Everyone under 15 today will reach working age within the next 15 years.

What the comparison shows

Adding Africa to the Journal's comparison changes the picture in two ways. On the headline measure, 16 of the 51 African countries with estimates report youth unemployment above France's 18.9%, the highest rate among the five countries the Journal charted. In much of the rest of the continent, the headline measure says little about the quality of work: Madagascar and Tanzania both report youth unemployment below Germany's, yet 84.4% and 83.7% of their workers are in vulnerable employment.

These are descriptive comparisons, and they have limits. The figures are International Labour Organization modeled estimates, which are less reliable for countries with few recent labor-force surveys. Vulnerable employment covers workers of all ages, so it describes the labor market young people enter rather than young workers alone. The protest table places events beside labor data for seven countries; it does not test whether those conditions caused the protests.

For readers comparing countries, the unemployment rate is most informative when read alongside a measure of job quality such as vulnerable employment. Two gaps in the public data stand out. There is no annually updated, cross-country series on young adults living with their parents in Africa comparable to the Eurostat data the Journal used, and the World Bank series used here does not report vulnerable employment separately for young workers. This page will update as the World Bank revises its estimates.

Methods and data

Data. All labor and population figures come from the World Bank's World Development Indicators, retrieved through its public API. Youth unemployment and vulnerable employment are International Labour Organization modeled estimates, which fill gaps between national labor-force surveys. For countries with few recent surveys, such as Djibouti, Libya and Somalia, treat single-year values as approximate. Africa here means the World Bank's Sub-Saharan Africa region plus the six African members of its Middle East and North Africa region: Algeria, Djibouti, Egypt, Libya, Morocco and Tunisia. Of these 54 economies, 51 have a 2025 estimate; Seychelles, South Sudan and Sudan do not. Values are rounded half up to one decimal.

Definitions. Youth unemployment: people aged 15 to 24 without work who are available for and seeking work, as a share of the labor force of that age. Vulnerable employment: own-account workers plus contributing family workers, as a share of total employment, all ages (World Bank indicator SL.EMP.VULN.ZS, from ILO modeled estimates). Population under 15: people aged 0 to 14 as a share of the total population.

Updates. The data are checked monthly. When the World Bank revises its estimates, the changes are reviewed before publication, and the figures in this text update with them. World Bank figures last updated November 11, 2026.

What is missing. The original chart's companion graphic used Eurostat data on young adults living with their parents. No comparable, annually updated series exists across African countries, so it is not reproduced here.
