Africa's Youth Unemployment

A companion to The Wall Street Journal's charts on why young people are fed up with the economy, extending the same World Bank comparison to Africa.

By NELCRUM SolutionsNovember 2026Data refreshed monthly, most recently November 19, 2026

Key findings

  1. 16 of 51 African countries with estimates have higher youth unemployment than France (18.9%), the highest of the five countries in the Journal's charts.
  2. Low unemployment rates can mask insecure work. In all 19 African countries with youth unemployment below Germany's 6.9%, more than half of workers are self-employed without employees or work unpaid in a family business. The median share is 76.9%.
  3. 40.4% of Sub-Saharan Africa's population is under 15, against 14.2% in the European Union.
About this analysis

In a post on Instagram, The Wall Street Journal shared two charts under the headline “Young People Across the World Are Fed Up With the Economy.” One tracked youth unemployment from 1991 to 2025 in France, China, the U.K., the U.S. and Germany, using World Bank data. The other showed the share of young adults living with their parents in Italy, Spain, Ireland, France and the European Union, using Eurostat data.

Africa was not among the places charted, a gap raised in the comments on the post. Sub-Saharan Africa is the world's youngest region, with 40.4% of its population under 15. This article also looks at seven African countries where youth-led protests drew national or international attention in 2024 and 2025: Kenya, Nigeria, Togo, Morocco, Madagascar, Cameroon and Tanzania.

This analysis keeps the Journal's framing and its data source, the World Bank's youth unemployment estimates, and adds the 51 African countries with estimates so the figures can be read side by side. It also adds a measure of job quality, because a low unemployment rate can hide insecure work.

Of the five economies in the chart, France had the highest youth unemployment rate, at 18.9%. Among the 51 African countries with an estimate, 16 have a higher rate than France. South Africa's is 59.9%.

The whole continent

Ranked against the five economies in the original chart, Djibouti tops the list at 76.8%. The highest rates are concentrated in Southern Africa and North Africa, with the Republic of Congo, Gabon and Somalia also near the top. Many of the lowest are in West Africa, and the next chart shows what those low rates leave out.

Low unemployment, high precarity

Five of the seven protest countries have youth unemployment below Germany's 6.9%: Tanzania (2.4%), Togo (3.4%), Madagascar (5.2%), Nigeria (5.3%) and Cameroon (6.5%). That does not necessarily signal a healthy labor market. Under the international definition, anyone who worked for pay or profit for even one hour in the survey week counts as employed. The unemployment rate captures only people with no work at all who are looking for it. One likely reason the rates are so low is that where unemployment benefits are limited, few young people can afford to stay out of work while they search, so many take whatever informal work exists.

In Madagascar, President Andry Rajoelina fled in October 2025 after soldiers joined weeks of youth-led protests, and the military took power. Youth unemployment there is 5.2%, below Germany's 6.9%. A second measure gives context: 84.4% of workers of all ages work for themselves without employees or help run a family business without pay. That figure describes the whole workforce. How many young workers are in such jobs is not measured.

What is vulnerable employment?

Vulnerable employment is the share of all workers in two groups. Own-account workers are self-employed with no employees: a street vendor, a smallholder farmer, a motorbike-taxi driver. Contributing family workers work unpaid in a business or farm run by a relative.

The International Labour Organization treats it as a gauge of precarious work. The World Bank's notes on the indicator describe these workers as the least likely to have formal work arrangements or social protection, and the least protected against economic shocks (World Bank definition).

It measures how people work, not how much they earn, and it covers workers of all ages, not only the young. A self-employed professional counts too, so read it as a rough indicator of precarious work, not an exact count.

The youngest region on earth

In Sub-Saharan Africa, 40.4% of the population is under 15. In the European Union the figure is 14.2%. That is about 534 million children, more than eight times the European Union's 64 million.

Africa's Gen Z protests, by the numbers

The table lists seven African countries where youth-led protests drew national or international attention in 2024 and 2025, with their labor-market conditions. It describes conditions alongside protests; it does not show that labor-market conditions caused them.

What the numbers add up to

Put the two measures side by side and they point in opposite directions. The 16 African countries with youth unemployment above France's 18.9% have a median of 30.1% of workers in vulnerable jobs. The 19 countries below Germany's 6.9% have a median of 76.9%, and in every one of them more than half of all workers hold vulnerable jobs. Across all 51 countries, the statistical correlation between the two measures is -0.77, where -1 would mean the two rankings are exact mirror images. So in many African countries, a low unemployment rate says little about whether young people have secure work.

The numbers carry caveats. They are estimates from the International Labour Organization, which uses statistical models to fill gaps where countries have not run recent labor surveys, so figures for places such as Djibouti, Libya and Somalia are rough. The vulnerable-work figures cover all workers, young and old. And the protest table shows what labor conditions looked like in the countries where protests broke out; it does not prove those conditions caused them.

For anyone comparing countries, the takeaway is to read the unemployment rate alongside a measure of job quality such as vulnerable employment. Africa's numbers also have two blind spots. No regularly updated data across African countries shows how many young adults still live with their parents, the measure Eurostat publishes for Europe and the Journal charted. And the World Bank's figures on vulnerable work are broken down by sex but not by age, so they cannot show how many young workers in particular hold insecure jobs.

Methods and data

Data. All labor and population figures come from the World Bank's World Development Indicators, retrieved through its public API. Youth unemployment and vulnerable employment are International Labour Organization modeled estimates, which fill gaps between national labor-force surveys. For countries with few recent surveys, such as Djibouti, Libya and Somalia, treat single-year values as approximate. Africa here means the World Bank's Sub-Saharan Africa region plus the six African members of its Middle East and North Africa region: Algeria, Djibouti, Egypt, Libya, Morocco and Tunisia. Of these 54 economies, 51 have a 2025 estimate; Seychelles, South Sudan and Sudan do not. Values are rounded half up to one decimal. The correlation in the final section is a Spearman rank correlation across the 51 countries with a 2025 estimate; the medians cover the African countries above France's rate and below Germany's.

Definitions. Youth unemployment: people aged 15 to 24 without work who are available for and seeking work, as a share of the labor force of that age. Vulnerable employment: own-account workers plus contributing family workers, as a share of total employment, all ages (World Bank indicator SL.EMP.VULN.ZS, from ILO modeled estimates). Population under 15: people aged 0 to 14 as a share of the total population.

Updates. The data are checked monthly. When the World Bank revises its estimates, the changes are reviewed before publication, and the figures in this text update with them. World Bank figures last updated November 11, 2026.

What is missing. The original chart's companion graphic used Eurostat data on young adults living with their parents. No comparable, annually updated series exists across African countries, so it is not reproduced here.
